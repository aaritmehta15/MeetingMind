Krutarth, let's start with the fabricated 4-layer active balancing daughterboard and your lab measurements.

Krutarth: Thanks Aarit. The 4-layer active capacitive balancing PCB arrived from the fabrication facility last Tuesday, and I completed surface-mount component soldering and microscopic inspection in the power electronics lab. During our initial power-up test on a benchtop power supply, we encountered a high-frequency noise issue: the DC-DC buck converter was coupling roughly 85 millivolts of switching ripple onto our current sensing shunt resistor line. This caused erratic current readings on the ADC during high-load switching transients.

Aarit: How did you mitigate that switching noise, Krutarth?

Krutarth: I modified the front-end analog filtering network by adding a dedicated first-order RC low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency set at 1.2 kHz, along with high-frequency ferrite beads on both differential input lines to the ADS1115 16-bit analog-to-digital converter. After recalibrating the differential zero-offset in firmware, current measurement noise dropped from ±280 milliamps down to ±9 milliamps, achieving a precision of 0.08% of full scale.

Aarit: That is well within our 0.1% design requirement. What busbar interconnect method did we finalize for linking the 21700 cells in the 13S4P matrix?

Krutarth: We utilized 0.2-millimeter thick pure nickel strips with a slotted pattern, bonded using a dual-pulse capacitive discharge spot welder. Ultrasonic spot welding ensured that our contact resistance across every series link remained below 0.75 milliohms. Low contact resistance prevents localized I-squared-R resistive heating at high current discharge.

Aarit: Excellent hardware craft. Now, what were the experimental results for the active capacitive charge-shuttling circuit under an actual cell imbalance?

Krutarth: To rigorously test active balancing, we introduced an intentional 180 millivolt delta between Cell 4 (discharged to 3.72 volts) and Cell 9 (charged to 3.90 volts) in our 13-series pack. When we engaged the TI BQ76952 and our switched-capacitor flying capacitor network running at 50 kHz, the circuit continuously transferred charge from high-voltage cells to low-voltage cells. It equalized all 13 cell voltages to within 7.5 millivolts of each other in exactly 3 hours and 42 minutes.

Aarit: And how did that compare against our passive resistive baseline?

Krutarth: We ran the exact same 180 millivolt imbalance on a traditional passive bleeder resistor board. The passive board required 11 hours and 18 minutes to balance the pack, while generating 14.5 watts of continuous heat waste that heated the adjacent cells by 6.4°C. Our active capacitive balancer completed the task in one-third of the time and operated with an energetic transfer efficiency of 89.2%, producing virtually zero perceptible thermal dissipation.

Akshat: That confirms our CFD modeling predictions. The absence of localized resistor heat allowed our liquid cold-plate to maintain near-isothermal conditions across the entire cell array during balancing.

Aarit: Akshat, walk us through the experimental thermal validation tests you conducted with the DC electronic load.

Akshat: We placed the assembled 13S4P pack inside our 3D-printed ABS structural harness, clamped directly against the dual-pass aluminum cold-plate with 1.5-millimeter thermal conductive gap pads rated at 6.0 W/m-K. We connected the pack to our laboratory programmable DC electronic load and subjected it to a continuous 2.0C fast-charging stress test at 40 amps for 35 minutes. Our 12-volt brushless centrifugal pump circulated the 50/50 ethylene glycol and distilled water mixture at 2.4 liters per minute through the external radiator.

Aarit: Where did you place the temperature thermistors across the pack to capture hot spots?

Akshat: We mounted ten precision NTC 10k thermistors: six placed directly on the negative terminals of the core cells—which experience the highest internal Joule heating—two on the outer perimeter cells, and two monitoring coolant inlet and outlet temperatures.

Aarit: What were the maximum cell core temperatures and the fluid temperature delta?

Akshat: Ambient room temperature in the lab was 22.8°C. Coolant inlet temperature was measured at 24.2°C, and coolant outlet temperature reached 27.3°C, giving an inlet-to-outlet delta of just 3.1°C. The maximum core cell surface temperature peaked and plateaued at 38.4°C, well below our 40.0°C nominal threshold and significantly beneath our 45.0°C soft thermal derating trigger. Even when we injected a momentary 3.0C acceleration discharge pulse of 60 amps for 60 seconds, maximum cell temperature only reached 41.8°C before stabilizing.

Devanshu: What about the safety trip response time when you simulated an emergency cooling pump failure?

Akshat: We conducted a formal fault-injection test by intentionally disconnecting the 12V pump relay and feeding a simulated 60.0°C thermistor signal directly into the STM32 analog input. The microcontroller firmware detected the threshold breach and de-energized the high-voltage safety contactor in exactly 11.2 milliseconds. This comfortably satisfies the 15-millisecond specification outlined in the ISO 26262 ASIL-C automotive functional safety guidelines.

Aarit: That is a critical qualification milestone for our thesis defense. Now let's turn to Arhaan for the embedded machine learning validation results. Arhaan, how did the quantized models perform on the STM32F407 hardware?

Arhaan: Thanks Aarit. I flashed our 8-bit quantized Temporal Convolutional Network model binary onto the STM32F407 alongside your FreeRTOS telemetry tasks. We benchmarked hardware execution time using the internal hardware DWT cycle counter on the ARM Cortex-M4 running at 168 MHz. A full forward inference cycle takes exactly 14.2 milliseconds, requiring only 84 kilobytes of Flash memory and 17.6 kilobytes of SRAM.

Aarit: What accuracy did the online Extended Kalman Filter achieve under dynamic drive cycles?

Arhaan: We fed the EKF with experimental voltage and current telemetry logged during combined US06 and WLTP dynamic drive cycles. The online Extended Kalman Filter achieved a State-of-Charge estimation Root Mean Square Error of only 0.82%, completely eliminating the cumulative integration drift seen in Coulomb counting. Furthermore, our temperature-compensated OCV lookup table successfully corrected state estimates during vehicle idle intervals with zero current.

Aarit: Arhaan, how did you incorporate physical electrochemical constraints into the neural network loss function?

Arhaan: To ensure the model behaves realistically across seasonal temperature extremes from -10°C to +45°C, I integrated an Arrhenius activation energy term into the neural network training loss as a Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) penalty. This prevents non-physical capacity recoveries and accurately models elevated internal resistance growth under freezing winter temperatures.

Arhaan: Validated against our 600-cycle accelerated aging test data: our TCN model tracked capacity fade and cathode phase transition shifts with a Mean Absolute Error of only 1.08%. The network successfully forecasted the 80% capacity retirement knee-point within 24 charge-discharge cycles, with a 95% statistical confidence interval of plus or minus 18 cycles. Most importantly, when we simulated pack degradation across a multi-year light EV operational profile, combining our active capacitive balancing with Akshat's proactive thermal derating increased total projected pack cycle life from 1,240 cycles up to 1,835 cycles before reaching the 80% retirement boundary. That represents an overall lifespan extension of 48.0%.

Aarit: A 48.0% battery life extension is an extraordinary achievement for a college Final Year Project. In our CAN bus telemetry stream, I successfully mapped Arhaan's ML outputs into standard CAN 2.0B broadcast frames at 500 kbps: Packet 0x301 broadcasts pack voltage and current, 0x302 transmits individual cell voltages, 0x303 broadcasts the thermal matrix, and 0x304 broadcasts real-time SoC, SoH, and RUL predictions every 100 milliseconds.

Krutarth: Did we verify that the 50 kHz flying capacitor switching didn't induce electromagnetic interference into the CAN differential lines?

Aarit: We ran an oscilloscope FFT spectrum analysis across CAN high and CAN low. CAN frame error counters remained at exactly zero throughout our 35-minute full-load test.

Akshat: On the cooling side, I also verified coolant fluid integrity. After 200 hours of continuous pumping through the aluminum cold plate and radiator, we tested the 50/50 water-glycol mixture: fluid pH remained neutral at 7.8, showing zero galvanic corrosion or aluminum pitting.
